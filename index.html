SiC dual-active-bridge converter for vehicle-to-grid

A MATLAB study of a dual active bridge (DAB), the DC-DC stage that can move power both ways between an EV battery and the grid. My simulations ran the battery-to-bus direction.

Status
Simulation No hardware.
Type
Independent study
When
Jul 2026
Tools
MATLAB

The problem

In vehicle-to-grid, the car battery charges and discharges more often, so it ages faster. I wanted to see if a smarter controller could control the converter well and also go easier on the battery.

What I did

  • Wrote a design calculator for a 3.3 kW DAB: 400 V DC bus, 250–450 V battery, 50 kHz, 1 : 1 transformer, eight 1200 V SiC MOSFETs (C3M0065100K). It sizes the inductor (58.9 µH, 30° phase shift at rated power), checks zero-voltage switching, and estimates losses and temperature.
  • Wrote a model predictive controller (MPC) that adds a battery-aging cost, and compared it with a normal PI controller over a 0.5 s run with load steps from 0.5 to 3.3 kW (Fig. 1.1). It is an averaged model, not a switch-by-switch one.
  • Started a simple detector for wrong sensor readings (Mahalanobis distance). It still misses phase-shift faults, so it is work in progress.

Results (what the scripts give when run)

CheckResult
Overshoot after a load step, PI → MPC17.5 % → 11.3 %
Battery aging, MPC vs PI (0.5 s run)only 1.5 % lower
MPC steady-state voltage error0.19 V
Calculated efficiency at 3.3 kW86.6 % (target was 97 %)
Estimated MOSFET junction temperature≈ 195 °C with the assumed heatsink (limit 150 °C)

What I learned

The design does not meet its targets yet. Losses are too high and the switches would overheat, so the magnetics and cooling need work. The MPC cuts overshoot but barely helps battery life in this test, and at 500 W (burst mode) it holds the output near 415 V instead of 400 V. I would rather show this honestly than show a nice number I cannot back up.

Solar charger with MPPT, for an off-grid sign

A 20 kHz buck converter with perturb-and-observe (P&O) maximum power point tracking, charging a 25.6 V LiFePO4 battery from six 540 W panels.

Status
Simulation MATLAB/Simulink
Team
Team Illuminators, InUnity Entrepreneurial Mindset Program. Our team was selected for the proof-of-concept round.
When
Oct 2026
Tools
MATLAB/Simulink, plus a sizing tool that runs on NASA POWER weather data

The problem

A small business wanted its outdoor signs to stay lit at night without grid power. Two questions: how big must the panel and battery be so the sign never goes dark, and how do we get the most power out of the panels when the sun keeps changing?

What I did

  • Built a sizing tool that runs the sign's battery night by night over 10 years of NASA POWER weather data (2015–2024, 9 places). In Pune, July gives less than half of April's sun: 3.2 vs 6.9 kWh/m² a day on flat ground. So the panel must be sized for the monsoon, not for summer.
  • The tool can also add a wind turbine, but in the city it never picks one. A turbine there makes only about 36 kWh a year. A bigger panel with smart dimming gave zero dark nights in 10 years.
  • Simulated the charger in Simulink: a 20 kHz buck converter whose duty ratio is set by a P&O MPPT. My first version with Simscape device blocks did not work, so I wrote the switch as circuit equations in MATLAB Function blocks.

Results (simulation)

Why 3,038 W and not 6 × 540 = 3,240 W? The model runs the panels at 45 °C, and hot panels give less power than their label.

The problem

A 72 V system with up to 125 A can hurt the driver or start a fire if a fault is not handled. FKDC rules ask for kill switches, a brake over-travel switch, isolation relays, an active light and a ready-to-drive sound. The main rule I kept in mind: any fault must cut motor power, and this must not depend on software.

What I did

  • Made a simple Simulink block model of the kart's electrical system (Fig. 3.1). The top half is the power path: two 72 V, 42 Ah packs in parallel, 90 A and 150 A fuses, a 200 A contactor, the motor controller and the 6 kW PMSM. The bottom half is the 12 V side: two kill switches, a key switch, the brake limit switch, the brake light and the ready-to-drive sounder.
  • Designed the 12 V safety loop: shutdown circuit, two isolation relays, brake over-travel switch and a ready-to-drive buzzer that sounds for 2 s before the throttle is enabled.

Step-down chopper on a breadboard

An Arduino-controlled chopper that steps 24 V DC down. Turn a knob, change the duty ratio, and the output voltage follows: Vo = D × Vin.

Status
Hardware Built and tested on a breadboard, first on a 9 V battery, then at 24 V.
Type
TE power electronics lab, group build
When
Oct 2026
Parts
Arduino Uno (PWM on pin 9), IRLB8748 logic-level MOSFET, SB560 Schottky diode, 470 µF 50 V capacitor, 10 kΩ pot; a 9 V battery for the first test, then a 24 V DC supply

The circuit

+24 V ──┬───────────┬───────────┐
        │           │           │
     470 µF       LOAD        SB560
      50 V          │      (band to +24 V)
        │           ├───────────┘
        │           │ D
        │       IRLB8748 ── G
        │           │ S
GND ────┴───────────┴────── Arduino GND (must be joined)

Arduino pin 9 ── 220 Ω ──┬── G
                         │
                       10 kΩ
                         │
                        GND
  • The MOSFET is the switch. When it is on, the full 24 V is across the load. When it is off, the load gets nothing. So the average output is D × 24 V.
  • The Arduino reads the pot on A0 and sets the PWM duty ratio on pin 9 from 0 to 100 %.
  • The SB560 diode is the freewheel path. With a motor as the load, the motor current needs somewhere to go when the switch turns off. Without the diode, that current makes a voltage spike that can kill the MOSFET.
  • The 220 Ω resistor limits the current spike into the MOSFET gate. The 10 kΩ pull-down keeps the MOSFET off while the Arduino starts up and pin 9 is floating.
  • The 470 µF capacitor holds the 24 V steady when the switch pulls current in pulses.

Readings

Supply VinDuty ratio DExpected Vo = D × VinMeasured Vo
8.1 V (9 V battery)≈ 0.50≈ 4.05 V4.03 V

What went wrong, and how we fixed it

  • At first nothing switched. The Arduino GND and the supply GND were not joined, so the gate signal had no reference. Joining the grounds fixed it.
  • One PWM wire was in the Arduino's power header instead of pin 9. Easy to miss on a crowded breadboard; now I check every lead against the pin guide before power-on.

Next

MineAdapt-EC: cleaning mine water with electricity

A modular, solar-powered electrocoagulation skid with on-device water-quality monitoring, for mine-affected water in Jharkhand. Smart India Hackathon 2026.

Status
DesignSoftware One beaker bench test so far. Firmware written and flashed; probes not yet tested in water.
Event
SIH 2026, problem statement SIH26040 (Govt. of Jharkhand). Team H2ohmies.
My role
Research and software
When
Sep 2026
Tools
ESP32-C6 with PlatformIO, Python
Result
Cleared the college-level round. National result awaited.

The problem

Water near mines in Jharkhand can carry iron, fluoride and acid mine drainage. Electrocoagulation (EC) cleans it with electricity: a DC current goes through metal plates, the plates slowly dissolve, and the metal forms small lumps (flocs) that grab the dirt. No chemicals have to be bought. But almost every EC cell in research papers is a batch tank in a lab. Running one non-stop at village scale is still an open problem.

What I did

  • Sized the reactor with Faraday's law. At 1.2 A and 0.5 L/min, the mild-steel plates release 41.7 mg of iron per litre. If the full 24 V bus sat across the cell, that would be 0.96 kWh per m³ of water; the real cell voltage is lower, so this is an upper limit.
  • In our team's beaker bench test (22.6 V, 2.57 A), floc formed as expected.
  • Wrote and flashed ESP32-C6 firmware that reads inlet and outlet pH, turbidity, TDS and temperature, and serves a live dashboard from its own Wi-Fi access point, with no internet needed.
  • Our team designed the skid as seven slide-in cartridges. As a cartridge slides in, the rail catches it first, then the water couplings connect, and power and data connect last through pogo pins. So a half-inserted module is never live (Fig. 5.1).

Sodium-ion cell recovery test bench

My main project: a bench to bring sodium-ion cells back after they have been stored at 0 V. I am keeping the method private until I have real bench results.

Status
Simulation Bench build is next.
Type
TE seminar and group project
When
Sep 2026 – now
Tools
LTspice

Two circuit problems I solved in simulation

  • Sensor amplifier too slow. My first design was one LM358 stage with a gain of about 100. An LM358 has only about 1 MHz gain-bandwidth, so at that gain it gives only ×3.6 at 300 kHz. I split the gain into two ×10 stages with a faster op-amp: ×83.7 peak gain, −3 dB band 59.6–543 kHz.
  • Current source ringing. With real vendor op-amp and MOSFET models, the charging current source oscillated: a 10× current step peaked 87 % above its target and kept ringing. A 1 nF compensation capacitor fixed it: it now settles in 52 µs with no overshoot.

Machine learning on power and sensor data

Through my IIT Guwahati data science degree I also build ML models, mostly on power and sensor data. Each one is tagged with the kind of data it was trained on.

Gahan: filling cloud gaps in satellite chlorophyll data

Real data Smart India Hackathon 2026 (SIH26067), Team H2ohmies. I built most of the ML part. scikit-learn, NumPy, SciPy.

  • Clouds hide a lot of the satellite chlorophyll data over the sea. I trained a gradient-boosting regressor with 13 features to fill the gaps: ocean-model output, NOAA sea-surface temperature and fronts, nearby clear pixels, the pixel's average on other days, position and date.
  • To test it fairly, I laid real cloud shapes from other days over clear pixels, and kept the test day and its neighbours out of training.
  • Cyclone Dana 2024 (7,702 hidden pixels, 12 days): R² 0.87 on log chlorophyll, against 0.85 for the pixel's average, 0.67 for interpolation and 0.22 for the ocean model. On a live week of new data it got R² 0.80. On another case (BoBBLE) it lost to interpolation, so I switched it off there.

Power-grid fault classifier

Simulated data Personal project, Aug – Oct 2026. Python, PyTorch, scikit-learn.

  • Built a simulator for faults on 3-phase AC, 1-phase AC and bipolar HVDC lines, made about 150k labelled cases, and turned each into 15 features (RMS values, FFT-based symmetrical components, peak di/dt).
  • An MLP (15–256–128–64–16) names 16 fault types. On 15k new simulated cases it scored 88.9%: 14 classes were perfect, but every L1-N case was called L1-G.
  • The reason was my own simulator: it made those two faults identical. So the fix is in the data, not a bigger model.

PredictiveGuard: motor fault detection

Simulated data Personal project, May 2026. FastAPI, scikit-learn, PyTorch, MQTT.

  • Wrote the chain ESP32 (MPU6050 vibration, INA219 current) → MQTT → FastAPI backend that computes 18 FFT and time-domain features for each 256-sample window. So far it runs with a simulated sensor node.
  • Compared random forest, gradient boosting, SVM, MLP and a voting ensemble, and added a PyTorch autoencoder for anomalies. All models tied at 23 of 24 test windows, so the simulated faults were too easy. Real motor data is next.
